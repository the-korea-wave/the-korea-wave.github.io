"The Odyssey" has become the second film of 2026 to pass 10 million admissions in South Korea, reaching the milestone 33 days after its release — and the enthusiasm has spilled well beyond the multiplex, with sales of related books up 31-fold in August compared with the same month a year earlier.

## A Second Entry in Korea's Most Exclusive Club

In the Korean market, 10 million admissions is less a box-office statistic than a cultural designation. With a national population of roughly 51 million, crossing the line means something close to one in five people in the country bought a ticket. Only a handful of titles achieve it in a typical year, and in weaker years none do at all.

That "The Odyssey" needed 33 days to get there says something about the shape of its run. Films that sprint to the milestone typically ride an explosive opening; a slower climb over a month points instead to sustained word-of-mouth and repeat viewing — the kind of long-tail demand that Korean exhibitors have found increasingly rare since the pandemic reshaped theatergoing habits. For a year that has now produced two 10-million films, the result strengthens the case that Korean audiences will still turn out in force for event-scale theatrical releases, even as overall admissions remain below their pre-2020 norms.

## From Ticket Stubs to Bookshelves

The more unusual indicator is happening in bookstores. Sales of titles connected to the film — led by editions of the ancient Greek epic it draws on, along with related classics and mythology reading — rose 31-fold from the same month last year, according to figures reported this week.

Screen-to-page halo effects are well documented in Korea's publishing market: a hit drama or film routinely sends its source novel up the bestseller lists for a few weeks. A 31-fold jump, however, is an outlier even by those standards, and it is notable that the beneficiary is not a contemporary novel but a foundational work of Western literature that most readers encounter, if at all, as a school assignment. The film has effectively functioned as a mass marketing campaign for a text that publishers could never have promoted to this audience on their own.

## Why the Spillover Matters

For the content industry, the pattern is a reminder that theatrical blockbusters still do something streaming rarely replicates: they create a shared, time-concentrated cultural moment large enough to move adjacent markets. Publishers, in this case, captured demand they did not generate. Exhibitors, for their part, get a marquee data point to bring to studios weighing whether Korea still merits wide, long theatrical windows.

The open question is durability. Book-sales spikes tied to screen hits tend to decay quickly once a film leaves theaters, and admissions milestones say little about how the title performs in later windows. But as a snapshot of September 2026, the numbers are unambiguous: a month-long theatrical run has minted the year's second 10-million-admissions film and, along the way, turned a nearly 3,000-year-old poem into one of the fastest-growing categories in Korean bookstores.
